# Dialogue Guide: Character Voice in Conversation

This document provides patterns and rules for writing dialogue that differentiates characters naturally while maintaining accessibility and readability.

## Core Dialogue Principles

### The Seven Rules

1. **Tags:** Use "said" as default, action beats preferred
2. **Subtext:** Characters don't state the obvious
3. **Interruption:** People talk over each other naturally
4. **Silence:** Not answering is an answer
5. **Rhythm:** Vary sentence length for natural flow
6. **Dialect:** Suggest through word choice, not phonetic spelling
7. **Exposition:** Never explain what both speakers know

### Voice Differentiation by Type

Characters need distinct voices that readers can identify without tags. Differentiate through:

- **Word choice** (vocabulary level, formality)
- **Sentence structure** (simple vs. complex, complete vs. fragments)
- **Speech patterns** (questions, statements, commands)
- **Topics** (what they notice and mention)
- **Subtext** (direct vs. evasive, honest vs. manipulative)

## Voice Templates by Character Type

### POV Character (Modern, Sardonic)

**Characteristics:**
- Modern contractions and vocabulary
- Self-aware, often self-deprecating
- Questions authority casually
- Humor as defense mechanism
- Direct but not blunt

**Dialogue Pattern:**
```
"That's a terrible plan," I said.

She raised an eyebrow. "You have a better one?"

"Not really. But I feel obligated to point out when we're
about to do something catastrophically stupid."

"Noted. We're doing it anyway."

"Wouldn't expect anything else."
```

**Key Elements:**
- Contractions (that's, we're, wouldn't)
- Casual criticism accepted
- Acknowledges futility but speaks anyway
- Banter maintains relationship
- Short exchanges, quick rhythm

**Common Phrases:**
- "Fair enough"
- "Could be worse"
- "Not my first choice, but..."
- "You're not wrong"
- "I've seen worse ideas. Not many, but some."

**Avoid:**
- Over-explaining
- Formal constructions
- Pompous vocabulary
- Taking self too seriously
- Dramatic declarations

### Ancient Being (Formal, Mythic)

**Characteristics:**
- Archaic constructions occasional, not constant
- Formal vocabulary naturally used
- Longer sentences, measured rhythm
- Speaks in complete thoughts
- Weight behind every word

**Dialogue Pattern:**
```
"You ask much of me, kinsman," Elrond said. "The road you
would walk leads into darkness, and I have seen too many
bright souls lost to such paths. Yet I will not forbid you,
for that is not my right. Each must choose their own doom."

He paused, and in the silence I felt the weight of his years.

"But know this—should you fall, the world falls with you."
```

**Key Elements:**
- Inverted syntax sparingly ("You ask much")
- No contractions in formal moments
- Archaic word choice earned ("kinsman," "doom")
- Pauses for weight
- Speaks in complete paragraphs when serious

**Common Phrases:**
- "Long have I..."
- "Yet it may be that..."
- "Such was the way of..."
- "In the elder days..."
- "Doom" (fate), "kinsman" (relative)

**Avoid:**
- Constant "thee/thou" (save for ceremonial)
- Incomprehensible archaic vocabulary
- Talking like a Renaissance fair
- Every sentence inverted
- Forgetting they're still a person

### Common Folk (Colloquial, Direct)

**Characteristics:**
- Regional flavor in word choice
- Informal contractions
- Practical concerns dominant
- Superstition mixed with common sense
- Less formal education apparent

**Dialogue Pattern:**
```
"Don't go up the Old Road after dark," the innkeeper said.
"Not unless you want trouble."

"What kind of trouble?"

She shrugged. "The kind that don't leave bodies to find.
My gran used to say the road remembers what walked it in
the old days, and some things don't take kindly to being
forgotten."

"Your gran sounds like she told good stories."

"She did. But this ain't a story."
```

**Key Elements:**
- Contractions frequent (don't, ain't)
- Folklore delivered matter-of-fact
- Practical warnings, not dramatics
- Family wisdom cited
- Regional vocabulary ("don't leave bodies")

**Common Phrases:**
- "Mark my words"
- "Beggin' your pardon"
- "As my [relation] used to say"
- "I ain't saying/I'm just saying"
- "'Twere" (it was), "ye" (you) - sparingly

**Avoid:**
- Phonetic spelling (sez, wuz, gonna)
- Over-the-top accent
- Making them stupid
- Treating folklore as quaint
- Forgetting they have agency

### Antagonist (Precise, Economical)

**Characteristics:**
- No wasted words
- Threatening through implication
- Cold logic or hot passion, rarely mixed
- Power demonstrated, not stated
- Questions are weapons

**Dialogue Pattern:**
```
"You've come a long way to die," he said.

I didn't answer. He wasn't looking for one.

"The Pattern believes it has chosen well. I wonder if the
Pattern can be disappointed." He circled slowly, a predator
considering angles. "Tell me—when did you last walk it?"

Trap question. Answering proved I had. Not answering proved
I knew it was a trap.

"Three days ago," I said.

"Liar. The burns are older. You're weaker than you're
pretending."

He was right. I was in trouble.
```

**Key Elements:**
- Statements disguised as observations
- Questions probe for weakness
- Short sentences build tension
- No banter, all calculation
- Truth weaponized

**Common Phrases:**
- "Interesting" (threat, not curiosity)
- "You disappoint me"
- "I had expected more"
- "This changes nothing"
- "You know what happens next"

**Avoid:**
- Monologuing motivations
- Explaining evil plans
- Losing temper without purpose
- Being evil for evil's sake
- Forgetting they think they're right

## Dialogue Tags and Action Beats

### The "Said" Rule

Default to "said" for dialogue tags. It becomes invisible to readers.

**Good:**
```
"We need to leave," she said.
"Why?" I said.
"Because they're coming," she said.
```

**Bad:**
```
"We need to leave," she exclaimed.
"Why?" I queried.
"Because they're coming," she shouted frantically.
```

**Why:** Fancy tags draw attention to themselves. "Said" disappears, letting dialogue speak for itself.

### When to Break the Rule

Use alternative tags only when crucial information can't be conveyed otherwise:

- **Whispered** - volume matters to scene
- **Shouted** - ditto
- **Muttered** - clarity of speech matters
- **Lied** - POV knows and reader needs to

### Action Beats Over Tags

Preferred method: Replace tag with action that reveals character.

**Tag:**
```
"I don't believe you," she said angrily.
```

**Action Beat:**
```
"I don't believe you." She threw the letter on the table.
```

**Why:** Action shows emotion instead of telling it. Reader sees anger through behavior.

**Pattern:**
```
"[Dialogue]." [Character] [action revealing emotion/state].
```

**Examples:**
```
"Fine." I turned away.
"Please." Her voice cracked.
"No." He didn't look up from his work.
"Why not?" She leaned forward.
```

## Subtext and Implication

### Don't State What Both Speakers Know

**Bad (As-You-Know-Bob):**
```
"As you know, Father was killed in the battle three years
ago by Benedict, and ever since then, Mother has been trying
to hold the kingdom together while dealing with our uncle's
schemes to seize the throne."

"Yes, I remember all of that very well, since I was there
when it happened and have been living through the consequences
ever since."
```

**Good (Subtext):**
```
"Three years," she said.

I knew what she meant. Three years since Father fell. Three
years of Mother holding the kingdom together with both hands
while Uncle circled like a vulture.

"He's making his move," I said.

"Finally."
```

**Why:** Characters live in their world. They don't recap for each other. Information emerges through what they don't say.

### Conversation Topics Reveal Priority

Characters mention what matters to them.

**Warrior:**
```
"The gate's weak. Three good hits and it falls. They know it too—
see how they're positioning?"
```

**Scholar:**
```
"These fortifications follow the Numenorean pattern. Third Age,
I'd say, given the stonework. Fascinating how they've held."
```

**Merchant:**
```
"A siege. Wonderful. Do you know what this will do to grain prices?"
```

**Same Situation, Different Priorities:** Each character sees the world through their expertise and concerns.

## Natural Interruption and Overlap

Real conversations aren't turn-based. People interrupt, trail off, talk over each other.

### Interruption Patterns

**Em-dash for Cut-off:**
```
"But if we just—"

"No."

"You didn't let me—"

"I don't need to hear it. The answer is no."
```

**Ellipsis for Trailing Off:**
```
"I thought maybe we could..."

"Could what?"

"Never mind."
```

**Talking Over Each Other:**
```
"We should go north—"

"North is suicide. East through the—"

"—because the northern pass is clear this time of year, whereas—"

"—mountain road takes half the time if we—"

"Stop!" I held up both hands. "One at a time."
```

### Silence as Response

Not answering is a powerful answer.

**Pattern:**
```
"Did you kill him?" she asked.

I looked out the window. The sun was setting, painting the
sky red.

"I'll take that as a yes," she said quietly.
```

**Why:** Silence confesses what words won't. The POV's avoidance speaks volumes.

## Rhythm and Flow

### Vary Sentence Length

**Monotonous:**
```
"I need your help," he said. "The enemy is coming," he said.
"We can't win alone," he said. "Will you fight with us?" he said.
```

**Natural Rhythm:**
```
"I need your help." He met my eyes. "The enemy is coming,
and we can't win alone."

I waited.

"Will you fight with us?"
```

**Pattern:** Long-short-long or short-long-short creates natural speech rhythm.

### Match Rhythm to Emotion

**Calm/Formal (Longer Sentences):**
```
"The council has convened, as you requested, and we have
discussed the matter at length. Our decision is final."
```

**Angry/Urgent (Short Sentences):**
```
"No. Absolutely not. We're not doing this. You're insane
if you think I'm agreeing to this plan."
```

**Measured/Threatening (Mix):**
```
"You have two choices. Walk away now, and I'll forget you
were here. Or stay." He smiled. "Please stay."
```

## Dialect and Regional Voice

### Suggest, Don't Transcribe

**Bad (Phonetic Hell):**
```
"Oi sez ter me mate, oi sez, we gotter get outta 'ere 'fore
them blighters come back, roight?"
```

**Good (Word Choice + Minimal Markers):**
```
"I told me mate, I did—we gotta get out before them bastards
come back. Right?"
```

**Technique:**
- Word choice ("me mate" not "my friend")
- Grammar patterns ("I told him, I did" - affirming tag)
- Occasional contraction ('em, 'fore)
- Vocabulary choices (bastards, blighters)
- Readable at normal speed

### Regional Flavor Through Vocabulary

**Northern/Highland:**
- "Aye" instead of "yes"
- "Wee" for small
- "Ken" for know
- "Dinnae" for don't

**Southern/Rural:**
- "Reckon" for think
- "Ain't" acceptable
- "Fixing to" for about to
- "Yonder" for over there

**Urban/Port:**
- "Mate" for friend
- Sailing metaphors
- Trade language influence
- Mix of cultures in vocabulary

**Use Sparingly:** One or two markers per character establishes voice without becoming gimmick.

## Exposition Through Dialogue (How to Avoid It)

### The Problem

Characters shouldn't info-dump on each other.

**Bad:**
```
"As you know, the Pattern of Amber was created ten thousand
years ago by Dworkin Barimen using the blood of the Unicorn,
and it grants our family the power to walk between shadows,
which are infinite reflections of the one true city of Amber."

"Yes, I know all of this because I am also a member of the
family and have walked the Pattern myself."
```

### Solutions

**Solution 1: External Audience**

Have a character who legitimately doesn't know, requiring explanation:

```
"What's the Pattern?" she asked.

Not someone from Amber, then. That was rare.

"It's... complicated. Short version: it's a maze that grants
power if you survive walking it."

"And if you don't survive?"

"You don't get to ask questions."
```

**Solution 2: Partial Information**

Characters reference shared knowledge obliquely:

```
"You've walked it," she said.

I showed her my hands. The burns were still visible.

"Three times."

I looked at her more carefully. "That should've killed you."

"Should have. Didn't."
```

**Solution 3: Disagreement About Facts**

Characters argue about interpretation, revealing information:

```
"Dworkin was mad when he drew it."

"He was enlightened. There's a difference."

"Enlightened people don't use their own blood as ink."

"The Unicorn's blood," she corrected. "Mostly."

"Oh, well that makes it perfectly sane."
```

**Solution 4: POV Internal Monologue**

Let the POV character think the exposition instead:

```
"The Pattern is calling you," she said.

I felt it too. A pull, faint but insistent. Damn thing never
left you alone once you'd walked it. Some said it was alive,
aware. I didn't know about that, but I knew it wanted something.

And I'd learned not to ignore it.
```

## Common Dialogue Anti-Patterns

### The Unbroken Monologue

**Problem:**
```
"You see, the issue is that we've been approaching this all
wrong from the beginning, because if you look at the historical
precedents, you'll notice that every time someone has tried
this strategy in the past, it has failed spectacularly, leading
to consequences that we're still dealing with generations later,
and I think we need to learn from those mistakes rather than
repeating them, which is why I'm proposing a completely different
approach that takes into account all of these factors."
```

**Solution - Break It Up:**
```
"You see the issue?" He leaned forward. "We're approaching
this all wrong."

"Historical precedent—"

"Exactly. Every time someone tries this strategy, it fails.
Spectacularly. And we're still dealing with the consequences
generations later."

He paused, waiting for objection. I didn't have one.

"We need to learn from those mistakes. Try a different approach."
```

### Idiot Ball Questioning

**Problem:**
```
"What's that big glowing thing in the sky?" asked the character
who grew up in this world and has seen the sun every day of
their life.
```

**Solution - POV Observation:**
```
The sun was setting, painting the western sky in shades of
blood and fire. Next to me, the outlander stared at it with
something like awe.

"It's beautiful," she breathed.

I'd never thought about it. The sun set every day. But watching
her watch it, I tried to see it fresh.

"Yeah," I said. "I guess it is."
```

### Constant Name Usage

**Problem:**
```
"John, I think we should go."
"Sarah, I disagree."
"John, please listen to reason."
"Sarah, you're being unreasonable."
```

**Solution:**
```
"We should go."
"I disagree."
"Please. Listen to reason."
"You're being unreasonable."
```

**Why:** People don't use names in every sentence. It's unnatural and draws attention.

### On-the-Nose Emotion

**Problem:**
```
"I'm so angry right now!" she said angrily.
```

**Solution:**
```
"Get out." Her voice was ice.

Or:

She didn't raise her voice. That was worse. "Get out."
```

**Why:** Show emotion through word choice, action, body language. Don't label it.

## Dialogue Checklist

Before finalizing dialogue:

- [ ] Each character has distinct voice (vocabulary, rhythm, patterns)
- [ ] No "As you know, Bob" exposition between knowledgeable characters
- [ ] Tags are "said" or action beats, not fancy alternatives
- [ ] Subtext present—characters don't state everything directly
- [ ] Interruptions and overlaps where natural
- [ ] Silence used as response when appropriate
- [ ] Sentence rhythm varies (not all same length)
- [ ] Dialect suggested through word choice, not phonetic spelling
- [ ] No unbroken monologues longer than 3-4 sentences
- [ ] Names not used in every exchange
- [ ] Emotion shown through speech patterns, not labeled
- [ ] Reading aloud sounds like real people talking
